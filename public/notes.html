There Is No Other God That Can Deliver

Daniel 1–7

The plan

One idea runs through everything: there is no other God that can deliver. A pagan king said that out loud after watching three teenagers walk out of a furnace (Daniel 3:29). Four stories, each one a hard choice made with everyone watching, each one ending with God showing up in a way nobody expected.

  • 18 slides, about 23½ minutes, which leaves a little buffer inside 25.
  • Keep the words simple. The slides put a plain-English line under every hard verse. Read the verse, then the plain line, and move on. You don't need to explain old words.
  • Out of time? Press W on the laptop (or click Wrap up) from any slide. It jumps to the "In short" slide, where you read the 2-minute summary, then the challenge and closing verse take another 2½ minutes.
  • Keep it moving. Each slide has one main question. Take two or three answers, then go on. Don't wait for everyone.
  • Running long? Use the cuts tagged If behind: skip the warm-up on slide 2, skip the "Two proud kings" slide (12), and read the Daniel 7 slide (15) without stopping to discuss. Those three cuts save about 4 minutes.

Five-minute prep

Read this once before class and you'll know enough to teach it.

The story so far. Last week Jeremiah warned Jerusalem that Babylon was coming. This week it has come. Babylon was the superpower of its day: the biggest, richest, most powerful city in the world. In 605 B.C. its king, Nebuchadnezzar, took a first group of captives from Jerusalem, including some of the city's smartest young people. Daniel and his three friends were among them, probably in their mid-teens. They were put through a three-year training program at the palace, given Babylonian names, and fed from the king's own table. The book covers about seventy years: Daniel is a teenager in chapter 1 and roughly eighty in chapter 6. Babylon falls to the Persians in chapter 5. Chapter 7 is a vision Daniel had years earlier, placed at the end of the story section.

What you'll tell. Four main stories (chapters 1, 2, 3, 6), two quick ones (4 and 5), and one vision (7). Everything you need to say is in the slide-by-slide notes below. Each story has one everyday example you can use: the party (chapter 1), the champion who gets beaten by the next champion (chapter 2), tryouts (chapter 3), brushing your teeth (chapter 6).

The one verse to have marked: Daniel 3:17–18, "our God whom we serve is able to deliver us… But if not…" If the class remembers one thing, it's those three words.

Title

30 sec
  1. "Last week Jeremiah warned Jerusalem that Babylon was coming. This week, it came. Today is about four teenagers who got taken to the biggest city in the world, and what happened when they wouldn't give in. The title is something a king who didn't even believe in God ended up saying: there is no other God that can deliver. Deliver just means rescue."

The only one in the room

1 min If behind: skip
  1. Both questions appear (two clicks).
  2. "Has anyone been the only one? The only one not laughing at the joke. The only one who left the party. The only one who didn't send the text. What made it hard?" Take one or two.
    • Everyone's looking at you
    • You don't want to seem judgy
    • You might lose friends
    • "I just left," "I'd already decided," "a friend backed me up"
  3. The gold line appears. Read it.
  4. "Hold onto that feeling. Daniel and his friends felt it at fifteen, and Daniel still felt it at eighty."
3

Carried off to Babylon

2 min
  1. "Babylon was the superpower of the world. Think of the biggest, richest, most powerful country today, and then its capital city. That's Babylon. Its king shows up at Jerusalem, takes the treasure, and takes the smartest young people back with him to train at his palace."
  2. The route draws across the map, about 900 miles, on foot.
  3. The four names appear.
  4. "Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, Azariah. Probably about your age. No parents. No church. Nobody from home watching what they did. Think about that: nobody would ever know."
  5. The cards flip to their Babylonian names.
  6. "First thing Babylon does: give them new names. Look at the old names. God is my judge. The Lord is gracious. Who is like God? The Lord has helped. Every one has God in it. The new names are all about Babylon's gods. It's like starting a new school where they hand you a new name and expect you to forget who you were."
  7. "What does the world try to rename in you?" One or two quick answers, or just let it hang and move on.
    • What's normal
    • What's cool
    • What "everyone" does
    • How you see yourself

Story One · The king's table

Slides 4–5 · 2¾ minutes · Daniel 1

4

Chapter: The king's table

15 sec
  1. "Their first test isn't dramatic. It's dinner."
5

Purposed in his heart

2½ min

The point is not vegetables. The point is that Daniel decided ahead of time.

  1. "But Daniel purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself with the portion of the king's meat, nor with the wine which he drank."
  2. "In plain words: Daniel decided ahead of time he wasn't going to eat the king's food or drink his wine. Picture the best food you've ever seen, served by the most powerful person alive, and everybody around you is eating it. Why would Daniel say no? Probably because it had been offered to idols, and it broke God's rules for his people. But notice the word purposed. He'd settled it before the plate was in front of him. And then he does something smart. He doesn't just refuse. He offers a test."
  3. The ten-day counter runs.
  4. "Prove thy servants, I beseech thee, ten days; and let them give us pulse to eat, and water to drink."
  5. "Prove means test. Pulse means plain food that grows from seeds: vegetables, grains, beans. Ten days on that and water, then compare us to the guys eating the king's food."
  6. The pulse plate glows and the king's plate fades.
  7. "Ten days later they look healthier than everyone else. And God made them wise. When the king tested them, they were ten times better than all his experts."
  8. The main idea.
  9. "Here's the thing to take home. Daniel decided before the moment. Deciding in the moment is hard: everyone's watching, you're tired, the food smells good. Deciding ahead of time means the choice is already made. It's like deciding you don't drink before you ever get to the party. Then when somebody hands you a cup, you don't have to think. You already know."
  10. "What's one thing you've already decided?" Take two or three. Affirm every single one.
    • Not drinking, not vaping
    • Serving a mission
    • Getting married in the temple
    • No pornography
    • Keeping the Sabbath, being honest
  11. If nobody answers, share one of yours, then ask again. Teenagers answer faster once an adult goes first.

Story Two · The statue and the stone

Slides 6–8 · 4¼ minutes · Daniel 2

6

Chapter: The statue and the stone

15 sec
  1. "Now the king has a dream nobody can explain."
7

The dream

2½ min

Nine clicks, but they go fast. Tell it like a story.

  1. "The king has a nightmare. He calls in all his wise men and says: tell me what I dreamed, and what it means. Not just the meaning. The dream itself. They say, nobody can do that. He says, then you all die. And that order includes Daniel and his friends."
  2. The prayer.
  3. "Daniel asks for one night. He goes home, gets his three friends, and they pray. That night God shows him the dream. Daniel says God 'revealeth the deep and secret things': God knows what nobody else can see."
  4. The statue introduction, then one click per metal (five clicks). Say each as it appears.
  5. "The king saw a giant statue. Head of gold: that's you, Babylon. Chest and arms of silver: the empire that beats you, Persia. Belly of brass: Greece. Legs of iron: Rome. Feet of iron mixed with clay: the kingdoms that broke apart after Rome. It's like a line of champions, each one beaten by the next. And the feet are the weak spot."
  6. The stone rolls from the mountain and the statue shatters.
  7. "A stone was cut out without hands, which smote the image… and the stone that smote the image became a great mountain, and filled the whole earth."
  8. "In plain words: a stone that no person made hits the statue, the whole thing crumbles to dust, and the stone grows until it fills the whole earth."
  9. The stone grows to fill the screen; the meaning appears.
  10. "The God of heaven shall set up a kingdom, which shall never be destroyed… and it shall stand for ever."
  11. "Every kingdom on that statue is gone. Babylon, Persia, Greece, Rome. You'd need a history book to find them. God's kingdom isn't gone."
8

The stone is still rolling

1½ min
  1. "So what's the stone? Here's the Lord in 1831, using Daniel's exact words." Read Doctrine and Covenants 65:2 on screen.
  2. "The stone is God's kingdom today: the Church of Jesus Christ. It started in 1830 with six members in a farmhouse. Now it's in almost every country on earth."
  3. The question.
  4. "Where have you seen the stone rolling?" Take two or three.
    • A friend or family member got baptized
    • New temples announced every conference
    • Missionaries in the family
    • Their own testimony growing
  5. Examples appear if they need a nudge.
  6. The gold line.
  7. "You're not watching the stone roll. You're part of it. Every time you keep a promise, invite a friend, or just show up, that's the stone moving."

Story Three · The furnace

Slides 9–11 · 4¼ minutes · Daniel 3

9

Chapter: The furnace

15 sec
  1. "The king just heard that God's kingdom would outlast his. His answer? Build a giant gold statue of his own, and make everyone bow to it."
10

Bow or stand

2 min

Spend your time on "but if not." It's the heart of the chapter.

  1. "The king builds a gold statue as tall as a nine-story building and calls in every leader from the whole empire. The rule is simple: when you hear the music, you bow. Anyone who stays standing goes into a furnace."
  2. The music plays and the crowd bows. Three gold figures stay standing.
  3. "The music plays. Thousands of people hit the ground. And three stay standing. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abed-nego. Think how visible that is. Everyone else is face down, and these three are standing up in plain sight. Somebody runs and tells the king."
  4. Their answer.
  5. "Our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods."
  6. "But if not."
  7. "In plain words: God can save us, and we think He will. But even if He doesn't, we still won't bow. Look at those three words. But if not. Their faith wasn't a deal with God: get us out of this and we'll be good. It was: we'll be good either way. It's like praying really hard to make the team, and still trusting God when you get cut."
  8. "What would change if your faith had a 'but if not' in it?"
    • Less scared about how things turn out
    • You stop bargaining with God
    • You can keep going when a prayer isn't answered the way you wanted
    • Your faith doesn't depend on good news
11

Into the fire

2 min

Let the fourth figure sit on the screen before you say anything.

  1. "The king is furious. He has the furnace heated seven times hotter than normal. It's so hot that the soldiers who throw them in die from the heat. The three are tied up and dropped in."
  2. The flames roar up and the three men drop in.
  3. A fourth, glowing figure appears.
  4. Wait. Let them look.
  5. "Did not we cast three men bound into the midst of the fire?… Lo, I see four men loose, walking in the midst of the fire, and they have no hurt; and the form of the fourth is like the Son of God."
  6. "The king jumps up. We threw in three. I see four. They're walking around. And the fourth one looks like the Son of God."
  7. Not even the smell of fire.
  8. "They walk out. Not a hair burned. Their clothes aren't even scorched. They don't smell like smoke. Now notice what God did not do. He didn't put the fire out. He didn't stop the king. He let them get thrown in, and then He was in there with them."
  9. The question.
  10. "When has the Lord been with you in a hard thing, instead of taking it away?" Go first with a short example of your own: a loss, an illness, a move, a hard year. The hard thing didn't disappear, but you weren't alone in it.
  11. The king's confession.
  12. "Blessed be the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abed-nego, who hath sent his angel, and delivered his servants that trusted in him… because there is no other God that can deliver after this sort."
  13. "That's the title of today's lesson, and a king who didn't believe in God is the one who said it."

Interlude · Two proud kings

Slide 12 · 1½ minutes · Daniel 4–5

12

Two proud kings

1½ min If behind: skip

Quick. Two stories in ninety seconds. Don't get pulled into details.

  1. "Two quick stories about pride. Pride is thinking you did it all yourself. Like someone who wins the game and forgets the coach, the team, and whoever drove them to practice. Nebuchadnezzar walks on his palace roof and says, 'Look at this great Babylon, that I built.'"
  2. The tree becomes a stump.
  3. "He'd had a dream about a huge tree chopped down to a stump, and Daniel had warned him what it meant. The second he says those words, it happens. He loses his mind and lives outside like an animal for seven years, until he finally looks up and admits God is in charge. Then his mind comes back, and he says God 'is able to abase' the proud. Abase means bring down."
  4. Belshazzar's feast.
  5. "Years later the new king, Belshazzar, throws a party for a thousand guests, and to show off he uses the sacred cups stolen from God's temple as party cups. And a hand appears and starts writing on the wall."
  6. The writing appears word by word.
  7. "Mene, mene, tekel, upharsin. Nobody can read it. They call in Daniel, who's an old man by now. He tells the king: you've been tested, and you failed. God has counted your days, and they're done."
  8. The city falls and the verse appears.
  9. "That same night the Persians took Babylon, and Belshazzar was dead. Same lesson both times: God is the one really in charge. One king was humbled and came back. The other was warned and ignored it."

Story Four · The lions' den

Slides 13–14 · 2¾ minutes · Daniel 6

13

Chapter: The lions' den

15 sec
  1. "New empire. New king. And Daniel is an old man now."
14

Three times a day

2½ min

"As he did aforetime" means "the same as always." That's the key phrase. The law didn't make Daniel brave; his habits did.

  1. "Daniel is about eighty. He's the king's right-hand man, and the other leaders hate it. They go digging for dirt on him and find nothing. The only way to get him is through his religion. So they talk the king into signing a law: for thirty days, nobody prays to anyone but the king. Break it, and it's the lions."
  2. Daniel 6:10.
  3. "He went into his house; and his windows being open… he kneeled upon his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did aforetime."
  4. "In plain words: he went home and prayed three times a day, the same as always. Windows open. Nothing hidden. And nothing changed. He didn't pray more to make a point. He didn't pray less to stay safe. He prayed exactly the way he'd prayed every day for sixty-five years."
  5. The stone rolls over the den and lions' eyes glow in the dark.
  6. "They catch him, of course. The king realizes he's been tricked, and he likes Daniel, but a law is a law. As they seal the den he says, 'Your God will save you.' Then he goes home and can't eat or sleep."
  7. Morning. The stone rolls away, light pours in, the lions lie down.
  8. "At first light the king runs to the den and calls out. And Daniel answers."
  9. "My God hath sent his angel, and hath shut the lions' mouths, that they have not hurt me."
  10. "They pull him out. Not a scratch. And the reason the scripture gives: 'because he believed in his God.'"
  11. The question.
  12. "Here's the point. Daniel's prayer was like brushing your teeth. You don't decide every night whether to do it. It's just what you do. That habit he built as a teenager was still there at eighty, and that's what saved him. Not a burst of courage in the moment. Sixty-five years of the same thing every morning, noon, and night."
  13. "What habit are you building right now that you want to still have at eighty?"
    • Praying every day
    • Reading scriptures
    • Church every week
    • Being honest
    • Keeping the Sabbath

The vision · Son of man

Son of man

1½ min If behind: read it, don't discuss
  1. "Daniel also saw visions of the future. In chapter 7 he sees how the story ends. Four beasts rise and fall, one empire after another, and then this."
  2. "I saw in the night visions, and, behold, one like the Son of man came with the clouds of heaven… And there was given him dominion, and glory, and a kingdom… his dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away."
  3. "In plain words: Daniel saw the Savior coming in the clouds, and He was given a kingdom that lasts forever."
  4. "Son of man."
  5. "Six hundred years later, Jesus used this name for Himself more than any other. He said the Son of man came to serve, 'and to give his life a ransom for many.' Daniel saw Him coming."
  6. "Ancient of days."
  7. "In the same vision the Son of man comes to 'the Ancient of days.' Modern revelation tells us that's Adam. Before Jesus comes again, Adam will gather his family at a place in Missouri called Adam-ondi-Ahman, and the Savior will come to them."
  8. The gold line.
  9. "Here's the whole book of Daniel in one sentence. The statue falls. The beasts fall. Babylon falls. Persia falls. You need a history book to find any of them now. Christ's kingdom is still growing, and it never ends."

Closing

Slides 16–18 · 3 minutes on time, about 4½ when wrapping up early

16

In short

30 sec · 2 min if wrapping up

On time? Point to the six lines on screen ("Here's everything in one slide") and press →.
Out of time? Press W from wherever you are to jump here, and read this aloud:

"Here's the whole lesson in two minutes. Babylon, the superpower of the world, carried off a group of smart teenagers from Jerusalem, including Daniel and his three friends. They got new names and the best food in the empire. Daniel had already decided, before the food was in front of him, that he wouldn't eat it. He asked for ten days on vegetables and water, and at the end he and his friends were healthier and ten times wiser than anyone in the palace. Decide before the moment comes.

"The king dreamed of a giant statue made of gold, silver, brass, and iron. Every part was a kingdom. A stone that no person made smashed it and grew to fill the whole earth. That stone is God's kingdom, the Church, and it's still rolling today. You're part of it.

"Then the king built a giant gold statue and ordered everyone to bow. Three friends stood. They told him God could save them, 'but if not,' they still wouldn't bow. They were thrown into a furnace heated seven times hotter, and the king saw four men walking in the fire. They came out without even smelling like smoke. God didn't keep them out of the fire. He got in it with them. The king said, 'There is no other God that can deliver after this sort.'

"Years later, Daniel, now about eighty, kept praying three times a day when a law said he couldn't. He was thrown to the lions, and God shut their mouths, because he believed in his God. The habit he built as a teenager was still there at eighty.

"And Daniel saw the end: every kingdom falls, and Jesus Christ comes in the clouds to rule a kingdom that never ends.

"So: decide now. Trust Him either way. Pray like you always have. He is the only God who can deliver, and He will be with you in the fire."

If they ask

Why wouldn't Daniel eat the king's food? Was it bad for him?
The scripture says he didn't want to "defile himself," which means make himself unclean. The food probably included meat that God's law said not to eat, and it had probably been offered to Babylon's idols first. The health result was a sign from God, not the reason. The point is deciding ahead of time. It's a fair comparison to our own Word of Wisdom.
Was the fourth man in the furnace Jesus?
The king said the fourth looked "like the Son of God," and later called him an angel (3:28). Many Latter-day Saints believe it was Jesus Christ before He was born; others say an angel He sent. Either way, the message is the same: God was in the fire with them.
Are the kingdoms in the statue really Babylon, Persia, Greece, and Rome?
That's the usual Latter-day Saint reading, and Daniel himself says the gold head is Nebuchadnezzar (2:38). The exact labels matter less than the point: every kingdom people build ends, and God's kingdom, restored in our day, will fill the earth (D&C 65:2).
How old was Daniel in the lions' den?
He was taken as a teenager in 605 B.C., and the den happens after Babylon fell in 539 B.C., so he was at least in his early eighties.
Who was Darius the Mede?
Historians aren't sure; he isn't named outside the Bible. Don't get stuck here. The story is about Daniel.
Why did God let them get thrown in at all?
Daniel 3:17–18 answers it before it happens: faith isn't a guarantee of rescue. Sometimes He takes the fire away, sometimes He walks through it with us. The friends were ready for either.
What happened to Daniel after chapter 7?
Chapters 8–12 are more visions about the future. He worked for the Persian kings into old age and, as far as we know, died in Babylon, never going home to Jerusalem.
